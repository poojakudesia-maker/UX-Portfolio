Context

Two clients, one rule: the workflow can't be wrong

For Deutsche Bank, the work covered which banking workflows, e.g. trade operations or client onboarding. For UK Police, which workflows, e.g. case or evidence management. Both had expert users, strict audit trails and legal accessibility requirements.

What I owned: UX direction, accessibility standards, and leading 20 engineers and developers on-site in London. What the team shipped: the redesigned applications.

Stakes & problem

Expert users were working around the software, not with it

Business problem. e.g. high error correction effort, long training time, audit exceptions

User problem. e.g. multi-screen data entry, unclear validation, no keyboard path for power users

Risk. In banking, a mis-keyed field becomes a reconciliation break. In policing, it can compromise a record used in court.

Research

Observing users at their desks changed the priorities

  • Finding 1: e.g. users re-keyed the same data across screens

    Decision it changed: e.g. single entry with carried-forward values

  • Finding 2: e.g. power users relied on keyboard only

    Decision it changed: full keyboard paths became a release requirement, which also met accessibility law.

Architecture & iteration

Fewer screens, validation at the point of entry

  • Call 1: main structural decision

    why, and what you rejected

  • Call 2: Accessibility as a build standard, not an audit

    As accessibility SME I wrote the standards the London engineering team built against.

Iteration. Something tested with users that changed.

Solution

A guided workflow with errors caught before submission

Accessibility proof

  • Keyboard

    Every task completable without a mouse.

  • Errors

    Messages linked to fields and announced to screen readers.

Impact

+22% user satisfaction across regulated workflows
